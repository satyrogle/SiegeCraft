# Compose the screen first, then generate inside it

The rejected pass failed at the method, not the details. It built three static screens with PixelLab's top down map tools, which are made for scrolling tile worlds, so no tuning of the tiles could have saved it. Every shipped game studied does the reverse. It composes each static screen as one scene, in one camera, one light and one palette, planned around where the fighters stand or where the logo goes. Your stages and camp put a straight down floor and straight down props under three quarter fighters, which pixel art teachers name as the classic mistake. Your menus pasted a logo on a dark strip, which none of the title screens studied does. The harder news is the tool. **PixelLab has no tool built for side or three quarter backdrops, hub scenes or title screens. Its biggest scene generator stops at 688 x 384, exactly half your stage each way.** No shipped game found credits PixelLab for environments. The one well reviewed precedent found, a game that disclosed AI backgrounds, used them only as a base and then repainted them heavily by hand. So the route that can work is composition first. Make a free blockout of each screen at 1x with real sprites on marked feet lines. Then let PixelLab fill that one canvas in overlapping inpaint windows, with one style reference and one locked palette. Then clean up by hand. Nobody has shown PixelLab holding a three quarter camera across those windows, so a two window test of about 20 generations comes before any full stage. If that test fails, the only route with shipped proof is a human pixel artist.

## Top down tiles under three quarter fighters broke the one camera rule

The stages broke three rules at once. **The floor and props looked straight down, while the fighters are turned and seen from a lower angle.** Raymond Schlitter, who writes as Slynyrd, says that in most cases projections should not be mixed in one scene. Even an odd projection looks fine, he says, if every element obeys it ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 3](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2018/3/14/pixelblog-3-graphical-projections-1)). Matej Jan calls scenes that mix them Frankenstein projections, where each object keeps its own perspective and nothing fits together ([Retronator](https://medium.com/retronator-magazine/game-developers-guide-to-graphical-projections-with-video-game-examples-part-1-introduction-aa3d051c137d)). Players spot it fast. On a top down asset pack with side facing characters, one itch.io user said the player looks like it is lying on the ground ([itch.io](https://itch.io/post/4524338)).

**A straight down floor also gives no depth cue.** Height on screen reads as distance only when the ground recedes toward a horizon, or when everything shares one three quarter cheat ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 11](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2018/11/16/pixelblog-11-landscape-pixeling)). So your back row could not read as further back. **And the tiles repeat.** At 64 px the stage is 21.5 by 12 cells, about 258 in all. A corner set for two terrains has 16 tiles, and only one of them is all sand ([Tiled manual](https://doc.mapeditor.org/en/stable/manual/terrain/)). With no variants, every inner cell is the same picture, and any mark in it forms a grid across the stage.

You can put a number on the camera clash. PixelLab puts its `high top-down` camera at about 35 degrees and its `low top-down` camera at about 20 ([PixelLab docs, Camera](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/options/camera)). Seen from that low, ground depth shrinks to about half or a third of its true size (the sine of the angle, my arithmetic). So under your fighters a round shadow should be an ellipse a third to half as tall as it is wide. A straight down Wang tile draws it as a full circle. Measure the squash off the fighters themselves, from how their feet and any shadow ellipse sit, and draw the floor to match. Tile size was not the problem. Your 120 to 190 px fighters stand two to three 64 px tiles tall, close to the one to two tiles in RPG Maker, LPC and Slynyrd's guides ([RPG Maker MZ help](https://rpgmakerofficial.com/product/MZ_help-en/01_11_01.html); [LPC style guide](https://lpc.opengameart.org/static/LPC-Style-Guide/build/styleguide.html); [Slynyrd Pixelblog 55](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2025/3/24/pixelblog-55-top-down-character-animation)). Bigger or smaller tiles would have changed nothing.

The scattered props failed as stickers. A prop looks pasted when its camera, light direction, palette, ground contact or scale differs from the fighters and the floor, and props generated one by one each bring their own. The LPC guide says props without shadows will not look part of the scene ([LPC style guide](https://lpc.opengameart.org/static/LPC-Style-Guide/build/styleguide.html)). A cutout prop cannot even carry a proper contact shadow. A shadow has to darken the ground it lands on, so on a transparent cutout it can only be a dark blob, which reads as a hole ([csaf, Verdant Props](https://csaf.itch.io/verdant-props)). Straight down props also show the tops of skulls and obelisks next to fighters seen from the front.

The camp made the same mistakes plus one more. It was built backwards. Its sand floor and cliff band were straight down, its station props came from separate generations at other angles, and the Pals are three quarter sprites. Every hub process found starts from the whole scene. Bloomtown's art director gives the order: the designer outlines the location, a concept sketch sets palette and mood, a block in follows, and only then come the points of interest ([Twin Sails dev diary](https://twin-sails.com/?p=1899)). Generating each station alone and fitting them together afterwards runs that order in reverse. That is why the stations looked "clamped together", in your words. A tiled sand floor is texture, not a place, and it had no zones, no landmark per Pal and no planned open ground.

The menus failed differently. Each was one generated illustration with the logo pasted on a dark strip. **None of the title screens viewed puts a strip or panel behind the menu.** The quiet zone is painted into the art, like the dark tree trunks behind Blasphemous's list or the empty sky inside Chained Echoes' ring of cloud ([Interface In Game, Blasphemous](https://interfaceingame.com/screenshots/blasphemous-main-menu/); [Chained Echoes title](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0dl1fBfneWg)). An illustration generated with no reserved quiet zone leaves nowhere for the logo to live, so it ends up pasted on.

PixelLab's map tools did exactly what they are built for. Its tileset tool offers top down and sidescroller types ([PixelLab docs, Create tileset](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/tools/create-tileset)). Its map model is trained on 16 px tiles, its guide uses a 128 x 128 canvas, and it calls maps over 256 x 256 experimental ([PixelLab guide, Create Map](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/guides/map-tiles)). Nothing in its map tooling is documented to make a composed view with a horizon. The pass asked a scrolling world tool for a stage picture. **Confidence: LIKELY.** The teaching sources and the shipped games agree, and the one apparent exception, GBA Fire Emblem, turns out to support the rule. I have not seen the rejected frames, so this reads them from their description.

## Shipped battle screens are composed around where the fighters stand

Battle backdrops come in three families. Old consoles built them from shared tiles with a palette per backdrop. **Final Fantasy VI has 56 backdrops, each with its own 48 colour palette, built from 75 graphics sets and 49 tile layouts** ([FF6 ROM map](https://www.ff6hacking.com/wiki/doku.php?id=ff3:ff3us:doc:asm:rom_map)). Pokémon Emerald has 10 battle environments, and almost every special arena is a new palette on shared tiles ([pokeemerald source](https://github.com/pret/pokeemerald/blob/master/src/battle_bg.c)). Painted games use big flat paintings. Darkest Dungeon's rooms are 1920 x 720 paintings, and its corridors are built from numbered 720 x 720 panels ([Steam modding thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/262060/discussions/4/1741106440019366509)). The third family has no separate backdrop at all. Sea of Stars fights in the world with no cut to a battlefield ([Sabotage press kit](https://sabotagestudio.com/presskits/sea-of-stars)). Octopath Traveler's makers say their pixel characters look like flat sheets of paper against 3D sets, and they used lighting tricks to make the two sit together ([Unreal Engine interview](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/developer-interviews/octopath-traveler-ii-builds-a-bigger-bolder-world-in-its-stunning-hd-2d-style)). Whatever the storage, **each screen is drawn for the battle camera.** FF6's tiles are storage, and what players see is a composed landscape. None of the static battle screens studied relies on parallax. Their life comes from sprites and effects.

The ground follows one of two plans. With a low camera there is a horizon. In the researcher's screenshot measurements, FF6's grass starts about 43 percent down the battle area. Unicorn Overlord's horizon sits 38 to 46 percent down, with floor filling the lower 55 to 60 percent ([FF6 screenshot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Final_Fantasy_VI_battle.png); [Unicorn Overlord screenshot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Unicorn_Overlord_battle_gameplay.jpg)). With a high camera there is no horizon, and floor, props and fighters all share one three quarter top down cheat, as in Sea of Stars and Chrono Trigger's field battles. GBA Fire Emblem looks like the exception, an overhead terrain picture behind side view fighters. But **its fighters stand on a separate bright platform drawn in their own view, with dark contact shadows, and the terrain behind is small and quiet.** Its houses are about 16 px tall beside fighters of 35 to 47 px ([FE8 screenshot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:SacredStonesFight.png)), and platforms are tied to terrain types ([FEUniverse](https://feuniverse.us/t/how-to-not-use-battle-platforms-in-specific-instances-fe8/12438)). So nothing in Fire Emblem is mixed. The platform is the near ground in the fighters' camera, and the terrain is a far layer that only names the place.

Fighters stay readable because the scene keeps its contrast away from them. Fighters carry the full light to dark range and the strongest colour. The ground right behind them sits in a narrow band with small, even texture, and far layers go flat. Measured on a GBA Fire Emblem frame, **the fighters span about two and a half times the light to dark range of the grass behind them** ([FE8 screenshot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:SacredStonesFight.png)). FF6's busy pine skyline reads as one flat dark shape with almost no inner contrast, which draws a hard horizon line ([FF6 screenshot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Final_Fantasy_VI_battle.png)). Slynyrd calls the rule atmospheric perspective. The nearest layer has the most colour and contrast, and each layer further back gets paler, hazier and closer to the sky colour. He adds that action games often need muted backgrounds so sprites read, while turn based battles can afford richer ones ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 62](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2026/5/27/pixelblog-62-landscape-backgrounds)). Kindred Siege is a busy real time auto battle, so it belongs on the muted side.

Modern pixel games also glue sprites to scenes with shared light. Octopath ties a point light to each effect so characters cast shadows onto the scene ([Unreal Engine spotlight](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/spotlights/octopath-traveler-s-hd-2d-art-style-and-story-make-for-a-jrpg-dream-come-true)), and Children of Morta shines high resolution lights on its pixel sprites ([TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/how-dead-mage-created-the-art-for-children-of-morta)). It is real work. At Sea of Stars one developer worked on lighting full time for five years ([MobileSyrup](https://mobilesyrup.com/2023/09/11/sea-of-stars-level-design-sabotage-studio-thierry-boulanger-interview/)). A shared fog, shadow and colour grade layer over stage and fighters is the cheap version (my inference).

The documented order is stage first, paint last. Slynyrd's battle backdrop starts by picking the canvas and laying solid horizontal bars for the horizon. Then he splits sky and ground into bands, adds far layers moving forward, textures the nearest ground, and adds haze and clouds last, all in 15 colours ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 62](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2026/5/27/pixelblog-62-landscape-backgrounds)). Ironhide sketches a stage's road map and playtests it before any final art ([Game World Observer](https://gameworldobserver.com/2018/12/06/ironhide-long-fans-love-kingdom-rush-will-continue-working)). A Battle Chasers background began as a thumbnail doodle ([PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/archive/2017/09/29/20-stunning-pieces-of-battle-chasers-nightwar-artwork-by-comic-book-legend-joe-madureira-and-his-team)). Darkest Dungeon's only artist wrote his style down as rules, so he could stay consistent and in effect art direct himself ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/the-gothic-sensibilities-of-darkest-dungeon/1100-6424880/)). **Your stage is big for pixel art.** At 1376 x 768 it has about 38 times the area of Slynyrd's 192 x 144 canvas and 27 times a GBA screen. Nothing in the sources shows a pixel game hand drawing one unique painting this size per region. The shipped answer at scale is one template, reused pieces and palette swaps.

Regions change identity, not layout. FF6 sorts its 56 backdrops into eight terrain families ([Final Fantasy Wiki, Dance](https://finalfantasy.fandom.com/wiki/Dance_%28Final_Fantasy_VI%29)). Darkest Dungeon gives each dungeon one main hue with related and opposite accents ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/the-gothic-sensibilities-of-darkest-dungeon/1100-6424880/)). Slynyrd's valley, desert and forest use one band formula with different colours and haze ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 62](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2026/5/27/pixelblog-62-landscape-backgrounds)). So a region reads in one screenshot from three things: its main hue, its far silhouette and its floor material. That fits your one second screenshot rule. Time of day or corruption variants can be palette swaps, the way Pokémon recolours its arenas.

### Tiles are storage for scrolling maps, not the look of one screen

Tiles exist to save memory and to give big scrolling worlds a grid for collision, pathfinding and level editors ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Games/Techniques/Tilemaps)). Tile based games switch to whole pictures for battle. **RPG Maker MZ builds field maps from 48 px tiles, but its battle backgrounds are 1000 x 740 images, a floor image and a wall image combined** ([RPG Maker MZ help](https://rpgmakerofficial.com/product/MZ_help-en/01_11_01.html)). Street Fighter II's stages were composed scenes that the arcade board stored as tiles ([Fabien Sanglard](https://fabiensanglard.net/cps1_gfx/index.html)). A stage drawn once and never scrolled gains nothing from a visible tile layer. Tiles earn a place in Kindred Siege only as a way to build many stages from shared pieces, or for a floor band drawn in the fighters' camera. If you use them, the fill tile must be the quietest in the set, with detail at the edges and in rare variants ([LPC style guide](https://lpc.opengameart.org/static/LPC-Style-Guide/build/styleguide.html)). Tiled's own example weights a decorative variant at 0.01, a hundred times rarer than a normal tile, and flips multiply the variants ([Tiled manual](https://doc.mapeditor.org/en/stable/manual/terrain/)). Rocks and debris go on their own layer over the ground instead of being baked into tiles ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Games/Techniques/Tilemaps)). PixelLab's Create tiles Pro makes tiles from 16 to 128 px in `low top-down` or `side` view with style images ([PixelLab docs, Create tiles Pro](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/tools/create-tiles-pro)), so a floor tile in the fighters' camera is possible. The top down Wang set is not that.

### Props stop looking like stickers when they share five things with the fighters

A prop belongs when it matches the fighters and the floor in camera, light, palette, ground contact and scale. LPC outlines tiles and objects in a darker shade of their own colour. Characters get near black outlines and their own palette so they stand out, and every prop gets a shadow ([LPC style guide](https://lpc.opengameart.org/static/LPC-Style-Guide/build/styleguide.html)). The Verdant pack ships each prop twice, once cut out and once grounded. The shadow takes its colour from the ground it falls on, so a shadow on sand never comes out green ([csaf, Verdant Props](https://csaf.itch.io/verdant-props)). Slynyrd draws furniture on the floor's own grid and scales stairs to the character sprites ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 35](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2021/11/30/pixelblog-35-top-down-interiors)). For your rock spires, totems, obelisks, braziers, skulls and skeletons, that means showing their fronts at the fighters' angle and lighting them from the side the fighters' baked light comes from. Their colours come from the stage palette, their contact shadows take the tint of the sand or stone under them, and a fighter silhouette is the ruler for their size. The fighters keep the strongest outline.

### Your stage wants a horizon near 40 percent and three feet lines a side

By analogy with FF6 and Unicorn Overlord, put the horizon or far band edge 35 to 45 percent down the stage, about 270 to 345 px. That leaves a floor about 420 to 500 px tall. Each side needs three feet lines, back highest and front lowest, all well below the horizon. For 120 to 190 px fighters, feet lines 60 to 100 px apart keep the back row readable. Heads may cross into the far band. Feet never should. These numbers are mine, not sourced.

Three templates keep everything in one camera. Template one is a low camera with a horizon, as in FF6 and Unicorn Overlord. Template two is a platform per army in front of a quiet terrain picture, the GBA Fire Emblem look. One Fire Emblem 7 fight even shows each side on its own terrain, which suits siege fights where attacker and defender stand on different ground ([FE7 screenshot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:FE_Rekka_English_gameplay.png)). Template three is a high camera field with no horizon, as in Sea of Stars. **My call is template one.** Unicorn Overlord is the closest documented match to your layout. Two squads face each other from left and right. Each has a front and a back rank of three. The fight plays out on its own from stats and player set behaviours, over a painted landscape with a receding floor ([Zatu review](https://zatu.com/blogs/reviews/unicorn-overload-review); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicorn_Overlord); [Unicorn Overlord screenshot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Unicorn_Overlord_battle_gameplay.jpg)). The horizon band also carries the far silhouette that names a region at a glance. It is still a taste call, so it belongs in one free mock with all three templates numbered side by side. **Confidence: LIKELY** for the layout, which rests on two games' screenshots.

## Good camps are one planned scene with a keeper and a landmark per station

Hubs come in two families. Walkable hubs reuse the game's own camera, as in Hades, Children of Morta, Moonlighter and Cult of the Lamb. Click hubs are one scene where buildings or tents are the buttons. Darkest Dungeon's Hamlet is a town of buildings you visit to use ([Darkest Dungeon wiki, Hamlet](https://darkestdungeon.wiki.gg/wiki/Hamlet)). The Banner Saga turns its camp functions into tents you click, with a leave button at the bottom ([Banner Saga wiki](https://bannersaga.fandom.com/wiki/Camp)). Kindred Siege points at Pals instead of walking an avatar, so it is a click hub. Even where players place their own buildings, as in Cult of the Lamb and Loop Hero, every building is still drawn in one camera and style for one ground ([Cult of the Lamb wiki](https://cult-of-the-lamb.fandom.com/wiki/Buildings); [Loop Hero wiki](https://loophero.fandom.com/wiki/Buildings)).

The hub's camera follows the fighters'. Darkest Dungeon dropped a planned top down exploration view because characters seen from above felt unrelatable, and going side view everywhere also cut its asset count ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/red-hook-studios-talks-about-the-creation-of-darkest-dungeon)). Hades is isometric throughout, House included ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hades_%28video_game%29)). Cult of the Lamb kept one mostly locked 2.5D camera, which pushed key things toward the back of the screen ([Unity blog](https://unity.com/blog/en/games/recipe-behind-smash-hit-cult-of-the-lamb)). For three quarter Pals the match is **one three quarter elevated scene, framed like a stage.** A flat side on panorama would clash unless its ground tilts to match them.

Each station gets its identity from a keeper and a landmark. In Hades, Hypnos watches the pool and the Broker sits in his stall. The Mirror of Night, the main upgrade station, stands several times Zagreus's height and dominates a wall ([Hades wiki, House of Hades](https://hades.fandom.com/wiki/House_of_Hades); [Hades wiki, Zagreus's Room](https://hades.fandom.com/wiki/Zagreus%27s_Room)). Uncle Ben's workshop in Children of Morta is a small room driven by gears and wheels ([Children of Morta wiki](https://childrenofmorta.fandom.com/wiki/The_Bergsons%27_Home)). Wildfrost marks its Icebreaker Cabin with a block of ice on top ([Wildfrost wiki](https://wildfrostwiki.com/Snowdwell)). Dead Cells shows unlocks as flasks hanging on chains ([Dead Cells wiki](https://deadcells.wiki.gg/wiki/Prisoners%27_Quarters)). Coherence comes from one camera, one grid and scale tied to the sprites, and one light that every asset was made for ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 35](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2021/11/30/pixelblog-35-top-down-interiors); [TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/how-dead-mage-created-the-art-for-children-of-morta)). Open space comes from giving each station its own zone, not from a bigger empty floor, the way Cult of the Lamb keeps its central dirt area clear for the Shrine ([Cult of the Lamb wiki](https://cult-of-the-lamb.fandom.com/wiki/Buildings)). A pretty hub is not enough on its own. One Wildfrost review called its town and its fights two disconnected parts ([GGRecon](https://www.ggrecon.com/reviews/wildfrost-review/)), so your camp has to show what each upgrade changes.

Hubs show progress in five ways. Buildings go from dark and run down to lit and welcoming in Darkest Dungeon ([Darkest Dungeon wiki, Town](https://darkestdungeon.wiki.gg/wiki/Town)), and Hades even sells lighting as an upgrade ([Hades wiki, House Contractor](https://hades.fandom.com/wiki/House_Contractor)). Empty plots become buildings, as with Darkest Dungeon's district plots ([Darkest Dungeon wiki, Districts](https://darkestdungeon.wiki.gg/wiki/Districts)) and Rogue Legacy 2's bare plot of land ([GGRecon guide](https://www.ggrecon.com/guides/rogue-legacy-2-skill-tree/)). People move in, as rescued characters do in Enter the Gungeon's Breach ([Gungeon wiki](https://enterthegungeon.fandom.com/wiki/The_Breach)). Trophies pile up, like Wildfrost's tribe flags. New rooms open, as in Hades. For four Pals, the cheapest strong signal is three art states per station: dark and bare, then lit, then fully kitted. Add trophies that match each upgrade, and plan empty plots into the first blockout.

Built the standard way, your camp starts as one brief naming the four stations and what each Pal does there. Next comes a grey block in of the whole camp at final size beside a Pal sprite. It holds four zones, paths, a centre and empty plots, under one palette and one key light. Each station's landmark is drawn into that one scene. Light and atmosphere go on after, and the Pals' work loops go into their zones last. **This is where AI earns its keep in the camp.** Each station's upgrade states can be inpainted in place inside the master scene, so they keep its light and ground (my inference, untested). PixelLab's MCP also lists an object state tool that makes variants of an existing object (its tool description, read 11 Oct, untested). Its output is still a standalone object, so it brings the sticker problem back unless it is redrawn into the scene.

## Title screens paint a quiet zone for the logo instead of adding a strip

Every title screen viewed has one focal subject that carries the premise, a logo drawn in the game's own visual language, and a short menu of one to five items. Two layouts cover them. In the centre stack, the list sits under the logo in a calm band of sky, sea, haze or black, as in Hollow Knight, Chained Echoes, Owlboy and Darkest Dungeon ([Hollow Knight menu](https://interfaceingame.com/screenshots/hollow-knight-main-menu/); [Chained Echoes title](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0dl1fBfneWg)). In the side split, the logo or list sits opposite the focal subject over the darkest or flattest third, as in Blasphemous and Dead Cells ([Interface In Game, Blasphemous](https://interfaceingame.com/screenshots/blasphemous-main-menu/); [Dead Cells title](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mI7wEmZqCK4)). Blasphemous gives its logo a separate press start screen, so its big character close up never fights the logo ([Blasphemous press screen](https://interfaceingame.com/screenshots/blasphemous-press-any-button/)).

The logo belongs because it follows the art's rules and repeats a motif from the scene. Sea of Stars turns the O into a crescent moon that overlaps the scene's moon ([Sea of Stars title](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5rkQcFF3XZ0)). Darkest Dungeon splits its wordmark with a torch, and torchlight is a core mechanic of that game ([Darkest Dungeon title](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J24d332tgAY); [Darkest Dungeon wiki, Light meter](https://darkestdungeon.wiki.gg/wiki/Light_meter)). Stardew paints its name on a wooden farm sign ([Stardew menu](https://interfaceingame.com/screenshots/stardew-valley-main-menu/)). Key art guides say to reserve a quiet, low detail area for type, with the hero off centre ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/create-a-game-key-art)). Legibility then comes from outlines and a strong selected state, not a panel. Blasphemous changes the colour and adds a small pointing hand, Chained Echoes puts a gold glow behind the choice, and Hollow Knight adds ornaments on both sides.

Title motion in pixel games is small, local and looped. Stardew drifts clouds, flies two birds, drops its menu in and hides clickable sprites in its logo ([Stardew Valley Wiki, Secrets](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Secrets)). One cheap method is whole pixel parallax loops whose speeds divide the canvas width ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 23](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2019/11/12/pixelblog-23-parallax-scrolling)). Another is colour cycling, where rotating palette entries animated rain, waves, fog and waterfalls in Mark Ferrari's scenes from one still image ([EffectGames](http://www.effectgames.com/effect/article-Old_School_Color_Cycling_with_HTML5.html)). Motion stays away from the menu text.

The art, the logo and the menu font share one pixel size. On lossless Blasphemous captures, the art, menu text and version text all step on about a 3 px grid. That matches the 640 x 360 base players report, shown at 3x ([Blasphemous menu capture](https://interfaceingame.com/screenshots/blasphemous-main-menu/); [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/774361/discussions/0/1638669204735629860)). Dead Cells and Octopath instead lay a deliberate high resolution UI over the pixel art. Mixed pixel sizes inside one screen read as mixels ([itch.io](https://itch.io/post/15697736)). Your rejected 480 x 300 menu size is a clean 3x of 1440 x 900, so it stays valid if the logo and font are drawn at that size too. It just reads chunkier than the 1x battles.

The title has three honest options. One is the camp itself. Sea of Stars' title centres on a campfire, which is where its party makes camp ([Sea of Stars title](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5rkQcFF3XZ0); [Sea of Stars wiki, Camping](https://seaofstars.fandom.com/wiki/Camping)). Children of Morta uses an overhead view of the family home instead of a normal menu ([Tech Gaming](https://www.tech-gaming.com/children-of-morta/)). Two is a place on the centre axis, like Darkest Dungeon's ruined estate. Three is a character close up opposite the list with the logo on its own screen, like Blasphemous, using your own leader or Pal art. **My call is the camp**, as long as its blockout keeps a calm band of night sky or dark ground for the logo. It reuses a scene that has to exist anyway, ties the title to the Pals, and changes as stations upgrade. That is the evolving title idea Hollow Knight uses, with menu styles unlocked by endings ([Hollow Knight Wiki](https://hollowknight.wiki/w/Menu_Styles_%28Hollow_Knight%29); [Giant Bomb](https://giantbomb.com/wiki/Concepts/Evolving_Title_Screen)). PixelLab's docs list no logo or title tool ([PixelLab docs](https://www.pixellab.ai/llms-full.txt)). Its MCP does list a font tool, `create_font`, that makes a pixel font as a TTF for 15 generations (tool description, untested). That can supply the menu font, but a logo with a motif still gets lettered by hand in Aseprite at the title's pixel size. **Confidence: GUESS** on the pick, since it depends on the camp having room for a logo.

## PixelLab can fill a composed canvas but cannot compose one

Start with the limits, all from PixelLab's own docs. **Nothing covers side view battle backdrops, title screens, menu backgrounds or parallax layers** ([PixelLab docs](https://www.pixellab.ai/llms-full.txt)). The biggest scene generator, Pro (`create_image_pro`), goes up to 688 x 384 at 16:9 and takes one style image plus up to four references, but has no camera setting ([PixelLab docs, Create image Pro](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/tools/create-sl-image-pro)). Pixen has a camera setting but stops at 512 x 512 in area, and PixFlux stops at 400 x 400 ([API v2 schema](https://api.pixellab.ai/v2/openapi.json)). PixelLab says its own camera control is weak and does better from an init image ([PixelLab docs, Camera](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/options/camera)). So PixelLab cannot frame your stage. It can fill one that is already framed.

PixelLab's own guide grows big images that way. Start from a rough sketch and make a base. Then grow it section by section with selections that overlap finished pixels, leave part of each selection unpainted as style context, and describe what sits in the middle ([PixelLab guide, Create Map](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/guides/map-tiles)). Inpaint v3 fills a selection of a bigger canvas, up to 512 px per side on tier 2, at 10 generations per use ([PixelLab docs, Inpaint v3](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/tools/inpaint-v3)). The levers for a consistent set are one style image on every call, a forced target palette ([PixelLab docs, Color](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/options/color)) and init images at strength 400 to 600 for variations ([PixelLab docs, Init image](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/options/init-image)). Reviewers still unify palettes and fix frames by hand ([GameDev AI Hub](https://gamedevaihub.com/retro-diffusion-vs-pixellab/)). Retro Diffusion is no escape. Most of its styles top out at 256 or 384 px ([Retro Diffusion API examples](https://github.com/Retro-Diffusion/api-examples)), and Scenario says pushing past native size duplicates pixels and bleeds colour ([Scenario](https://help.scenario.com/en/articles/retro-diffusion-models-the-essentials/)).

The risks are specific. Critics name two kinds of tell. Craft tells are pixel blocks that change size, stray pixels and half transparent edges. Scene tells are perspective, light and proportion that do not agree. In a thread about another AI sprite tool, a developer whose company shipped three pixel art games said matching style, lighting and camera perspective across AI output is near impossible ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44207304)). Players flagged one game's intro backgrounds as AI because objects made no physical sense, like a couch backed against a fireplace, and one said the AI disclosure stopped a purchase ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3074110/discussions/0/595137684887881384/)). Steam's rules, rewritten in January 2026, cover AI content that ships to players ([IndieForGames](https://indieforgames.com/steam-ai-disclosure-rules/)). Your PixelLab fighters already trigger that, so the added risk from AI scenes is visible tells on a screen people stare at for whole fights. The precedent is thin. **No shipped game found credits PixelLab for environments.** The Great Rebellion, 99 percent positive across 280 reviews, says it used AI images only as a base for some backgrounds and props, then heavily edited them by hand ([Totally Human Media](https://www.totallyhuman.io/blog/the-surprising-number-of-steam-games-that-use-genai)).

| Route | Verdict | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| A. One Pro image at 688 x 384, shown at 2x | Dead under your one pixel size rule | Stage pixels twice the fighters' size |
| B. One 1x canvas filled by inpaint windows over a blockout | Best AI route. GUESS until tested | Seams, drift, weak camera control |
| C. Blender render pixelised as final art | Dead | Speckle, not pixel art |
| C2. Blender greybox in the fighters' camera, used as B's blockout | LIKELY helps B | Setup time |
| D. Human pixel artist for one stage template and the camp | Only route with shipped proof | Cost and time not researched |

Route A is cheap and coherent, one generation with one light. But it makes the stage's pixels twice the size of the fighters'. Critics name mixed pixel sizes as an AI tell, and you rejected exactly that look on 7 Oct. Route C as final art dies for a similar reason. A pixelised painting comes out as speckle, as the raider did on 30 Sep with 23 percent lone pixels.

Route B is the AI route that keeps your rules. Keep one 1376 x 768 canvas at 1x in Aseprite. Lay the free blockout first, drawn in code like your earlier PIL mocks: horizon, feet lines, light and dark bands, a quiet floor and real fighters standing on it. Build one locked palette from the fighters' approved colours plus the scene's ramps. Fill the canvas with Inpaint v3 windows of up to 512 px, each overlapping about 64 px of finished pixels. Leave an approved fighter unpainted inside the first selection, so the model sees the target pixel style (untested). Six such windows cover the stage, about 60 generations before retries, around 1 percent of your 5,000 a month. A Pro seed at 688 x 384 can replace the first window and covers exactly a quarter of the stage, with a fighter frame as its style image. But PixelLab does not publish its price at that size, and its camera cannot be set. Then unify the palette in Aseprite on the stage layers only. PixelLab's colour reducer stops at 512 x 512 in area ([PixelLab docs, Reduce colors](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/tools/reduce-colors)), and cutting a composite with fighters in it changes approved sprites. Clean up by hand, split into sky, far, mid, floor and prop layers, and add ambient motion as small sprites. A Blender greybox rendered from the fighters' camera with their light direction makes a better blockout than a flat sketch, because camera control is PixelLab's admitted weak spot.

Shrink it before spending. Make one free blockout of one stage. Run one paid window over the middle band with real fighters on the feet lines, then a second window overlapping the first to test the seam. That is about 20 generations. If the floor squash, light side and palette match the fighters and the seam holds, scale up to the stage, the camp and the title, which all use the same pipeline. If it fails twice, the method is wrong, not the prompt, and route D is the answer.

Route D is the only one with shipped proof. Every hub and battle screen studied came from one hand or one written style. Hades' art director drew characters, environments and concepts in one pen and ink style ([MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/?p=216051)), and Darkest Dungeon's look was set and written down by its one artist ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/the-gothic-sensibilities-of-darkest-dungeon/1100-6424880/)). Cost and time for a hire were not researched. Either way, everything before the paint stays the same: blockout, camera, feet lines, palette and quiet zones.

## Conclusion

The stage, the camp and the title are one job, not three. Each is a static picture built around a reserved zone: feet lines and a quiet floor, Pal zones and empty plots, a calm band for the logo. One pipeline, one palette and one key light can serve all three, and one test settles all three. The pass went wrong by starting from parts, when every reference starts from the frame.

AI's place in that pipeline comes after the frame exists. It fills and it varies. It does not compose. The biggest payoff comes once one template is approved by eye: recolours and new far silhouettes per region inpainted into the same template, and station upgrade states inpainted in place. That is the economy FF6 and Pokémon got from shared tiles and palette swaps, moved to a newer tool. **Confidence: the diagnosis is LIKELY, the composition rules are LIKELY, and whether PixelLab can carry route B is a GUESS that about 20 generations will settle.**
